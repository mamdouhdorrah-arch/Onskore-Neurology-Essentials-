CVStCerebrovascular Stroke
Ischaemic & Haemorrhagic Stroke — Assessment, Classification & Management

Definition

  • A non-traumatic, focal vascular-induced injury of the CNS that typically results in permanent damage, manifesting as cerebral infarction, intracerebral haemorrhage, or subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Aetiology

  • Ischaemic stroke: arterial occlusion (thrombotic or embolic)
  • Haemorrhagic stroke: intracerebral or subarachnoid bleeding
  • Hypertension — strong association with lacunar strokes
  • Atrial fibrillation — cardioembolic source

Clinical Picture

  • Screening: FAST (Face/Arms/Speech/Time) — positive predictive value 78%
  • Medical: ROSIER score (>0 = stroke likely)
  • Oxford/Bamford Classification: TACI (15%), PACI (25%), LACI (25%), POCI (25%)
  • Lacunar strokes: isolated hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, or ataxic hemiparesis; sites: basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule
  • Artery-specific patterns: ACA → leg > arm weakness; MCA → arm > leg + aphasia; PCA → hemianopia with macular sparing
  • Hyperdense artery sign on CT — visible immediately
  • Ischaemic: hypodense area; Haemorrhagic: hyperdense area surrounded by oedema

Complications

  • Haemorrhagic transformation of ischaemic stroke
  • Cerebral oedema and raised ICP
  • Herniation / coning
  • Seizures

Management / Diagnosis

  • Non-contrast CT head — first-line investigation to exclude haemorrhage
  • Maintain blood glucose, hydration, O₂ saturation, temperature within normal limits
  • BP: do NOT lower in acute ischaemic stroke unless complications or thrombolysis considered; lower to <185/110 mmHg before thrombolysis
  • Aspirin 300 mg PO/PR asap once haemorrhage excluded
  • Statin if cholesterol >3.5 mmol/L (delay ≥48h due to haemorrhagic transformation risk)
  • Anticoagulation for AF: start after imaging excludes haemorrhage, usually not until 14 days post-stroke
  • Thrombolysis (alteplase/tenecteplase): within 4.5h; extended to 4.5–9h with CT/MR perfusion or DWI-FLAIR mismatch
  • Mechanical thrombectomy: within 6h for proximal anterior circulation occlusion; up to 24h for anterior or posterior circulation with salvageable tissue on imaging
  • Secondary prevention: Clopidogrel preferred over aspirin + dipyridamole; Carotid endarterectomy if stenosis >50% (NASCET), within 7 days
LMSLateral Medullary Syndrome
Wallenberg's Syndrome — PICA Occlusion

Definition

  • Lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg's syndrome) occurs following occlusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA).

Aetiology

  • Occlusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA)

Clinical Picture

  • Cerebellar features: ataxia, nystagmus
  • Brainstem features — ipsilateral: dysphagia, facial numbness, cranial nerve palsy (e.g. Horner's syndrome)
  • Contralateral: limb sensory loss (pain and temperature)
  • Similar syndrome from AICA (lateral pontine syndrome) adds ipsilateral facial paralysis and deafness

Complications

  • Dysphagia may lead to aspiration

Management / Diagnosis

  • Supportive care
  • Antiplatelet therapy
  • Speech/swallowing assessment for dysphagia
CADCADASIL
Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy

Definition

  • Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) — a rare genetic cause of multi-infarct dementia.

Aetiology

  • Autosomal dominant mutation (NOTCH3 gene)

Clinical Picture

  • Migraine (often early and prominent feature)
  • Multi-infarct dementia
  • Recurrent ischaemic events (strokes/TIAs)
  • MRI: typical extensive lesions in temporal poles and external capsule

Complications

  • Progressive dementia
  • Recurrent strokes

Management / Diagnosis

  • No specific disease-modifying treatment
  • Secondary stroke prevention (antiplatelet therapy)
  • Genetic counselling
TIATransient Ischaemic Attack
Brief focal neurological deficit from vascular cause without acute infarction

Definition

  • A transient episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischaemia, without acute infarction (tissue-based definition). Previously defined as symptoms lasting <24 hours (time-based).

Aetiology

  • Embolic (e.g. from carotid atheroma, atrial fibrillation)
  • Thrombotic (in-situ arterial stenosis)
  • Haemodynamic (reduced perfusion)

Clinical Picture

  • Sudden onset, focal neurological deficit that resolves typically within 1 hour
  • Unilateral weakness or sensory loss
  • Aphasia or dysarthria
  • Ataxia, vertigo, or loss of balance
  • Amaurosis fugax — sudden transient painless loss of vision in one eye (described as 'curtain falling')
  • Diplopia, homonymous hemianopia

Complications

  • High risk of subsequent stroke — particularly in first few days

Management / Diagnosis

  • Immediate: Aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated
  • Urgent specialist assessment within 24 hours
  • Exclude mimics: hypoglycaemia, intracranial haemorrhage
  • MRI (DWI + blood-sensitive sequences) preferred over CT to determine territory
  • DAPT (if low bleeding risk, within 24h): Clopidogrel (300mg load → 75mg od) + Aspirin (300mg → 75mg od for 21 days) → then clopidogrel monotherapy; consider PPI gastroprotection
  • Alternative DAPT: Ticagrelor + Clopidogrel
  • If DAPT not appropriate: clopidogrel 300mg load → 75mg od monotherapy
  • If AF: anticoagulate once haemorrhage excluded
  • High-intensity statin (e.g. atorvastatin 20–80mg) — reduce non-HDL cholesterol by >40%
  • Carotid duplex / CTA / MRA within 24h if candidate for carotid intervention
  • Carotid endarterectomy if stenosis >50% (NASCET), within 7 days
SAHSubarachnoid Haemorrhage
Blood in the subarachnoid space — spontaneous (aneurysmal) or traumatic

Definition

  • An intracranial haemorrhage defined as blood within the subarachnoid space (deep to the subarachnoid layer of the meninges).

Aetiology

  • Traumatic SAH — most common cause overall (head injury)
  • Spontaneous SAH: Intracranial (saccular 'berry') aneurysm (~85% of spontaneous cases)
  • Associations with berry aneurysms: hypertension, adult polycystic kidney disease, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, coarctation of the aorta
  • Arteriovenous malformation
  • Pituitary apoplexy
  • Mycotic (infective) aneurysm

Clinical Picture

  • Thunderclap headache — sudden, severe ('worst of my life'), occipital, peaking within 1–5 minutes
  • Possible sentinel headache in weeks prior
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Meningism (photophobia, neck stiffness)
  • Coma
  • Seizures
  • ECG changes including ST elevation (autonomic neural stimulation / catecholamines)

Complications

  • Re-bleeding (~10%, most common in first 12h, mortality up to 70%)
  • Hydrocephalus — treated with external ventricular drain or VP shunt
  • Vasospasm (delayed cerebral ischaemia) — typically 7–14 days after onset
  • Hyponatraemia — most commonly due to SIADH
  • Seizures

Management / Diagnosis

  • Non-contrast CT head — first-line investigation (blood in basal cisterns/sulci)
  • If CT normal <6h: consider alternative diagnosis (no LP needed)
  • If CT normal >6h: Lumbar puncture (≥12h post-onset) — look for xanthochromia
  • If SAH confirmed: CT intracranial angiogram ± digital subtraction angiogram to identify aneurysm/AVM
  • Urgent neurosurgical referral
  • Supportive: bed rest, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis, stop antithrombotics
  • Nimodipine (oral) — prevents vasospasm
  • If vasospasm develops: ensure euvolemia; consider vasopressor if symptoms persist
  • Coil embolisation (preferred) or craniotomy + clipping — within 24 hours
aSDHAcute Subdural Haematoma
Fresh blood in the subdural space — high-impact trauma

Definition

  • A collection of fresh blood within the subdural space (deep to the dura), most commonly caused by high-impact trauma. Often associated with other underlying brain injuries.

Aetiology

  • High-impact trauma
  • Often associated with other brain injuries (contusions, oedema)

Clinical Picture

  • Spectrum: incidental finding to severe coma and coning due to herniation
  • Altered mental status (mild confusion to deep coma)
  • Focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, aphasia)
  • Crescentic hyperdense collection on CT — not limited by suture lines
  • Mass effect with midline shift/herniation

Complications

  • Mass effect — midline shift, herniation
  • Coning
  • Associated traumatic brain injuries

Management / Diagnosis

  • CT head — first-line: crescentic, hyperdense, mass effect
  • Small/incidental: conservative observation
  • Large: surgical decompression (decompressive craniectomy, ICP monitoring)
cSDHChronic Subdural Haematoma
Slow collection of blood in subdural space over weeks–months

Definition

  • A collection of blood within the subdural space present for weeks to months, caused by rupture of small bridging veins with slow bleeding.

Aetiology

  • Rupture of small bridging veins in subdural space
  • Elderly and alcoholic patients — brain atrophy → fragile/taut bridging veins
  • Shaken baby syndrome (infants have fragile bridging veins)
  • Often no recalled specific head injury

Clinical Picture

  • Progressive history (weeks–months) of confusion, reduced consciousness, or neurological deficit
  • Headache, memory loss, personality changes
  • Crescentic hypodense collection on CT with mass effect
  • May present with fluctuating consciousness ('lucid interval')

Complications

  • Raised intracranial pressure / mass effect
  • Progressive neurological deterioration

Management / Diagnosis

  • CT head: crescentic, hypodense (dark), mass effect
  • Small/incidental with no neurological deficit: conservative observation (may resolve)
  • Confusion, neurological deficit, or severe imaging: surgical decompression with burr holes
IVTIntracranial Venous Thrombosis
Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis — sagittal, cavernous & lateral sinus

Definition

  • Cerebral infarction caused by venous thrombosis, much less common than arterial causes. 50% have isolated sagittal sinus thrombosis; remainder have coexistent lateral and cavernous sinus thromboses.

Aetiology

  • Prothrombotic states (oral contraceptive pill, pregnancy, thrombophilia)
  • Infection (e.g. sinusitis → cavernous sinus thrombosis)
  • Trauma
  • Neoplasia

Clinical Picture

  • Headache (may be sudden onset)
  • Nausea & vomiting
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Sagittal sinus: seizures, hemiplegia, parasagittal haemorrhagic infarctions, 'empty delta sign' on venography
  • Cavernous sinus: periorbital erythema/oedema, ophthalmoplegia (6th → 3rd/4th), trigeminal hyperaesthesia, central retinal vein thrombosis
  • Lateral sinus: 6th and 7th cranial nerve palsies

Complications

  • Cerebral infarction (may be haemorrhagic)

Management / Diagnosis

  • MRI venography — gold standard; CT venography as alternative
  • Non-contrast CT normal in ~70%
  • D-dimer may be elevated
  • Anticoagulation: LMWH acutely → warfarin long-term
TBIHead Injury / Traumatic Brain Injury
Types of intracranial injury — extradural, subdural, subarachnoid, intracerebral

Definition

  • Primary brain injury may be focal (contusion/haematoma) or diffuse (diffuse axonal injury from mechanical shearing/deceleration). Secondary brain injury occurs when cerebral oedema, ischaemia, infection, or herniation exacerbates the original injury.

Aetiology

  • Extradural (epidural) haematoma: middle meningeal artery rupture from temporal skull fracture; may have lucid interval
  • Subdural haematoma: bridging vein rupture; risk factors: old age, alcoholism, anticoagulation
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage: sudden occipital headache; spontaneous (aneurysm) or traumatic
  • Intracerebral haematoma: risk factors include hypertension, AVM, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, trauma, brain tumour, infarct
  • Diffuse axonal injury: deceleration/shearing of axons

Clinical Picture

  • Coup (adjacent) and contre-coup (contralateral) contusions
  • Features of raised ICP: headache, vomiting, altered consciousness
  • Cushing's reflex (hypertension + bradycardia) — late, pre-terminal event
  • Extradural: may have lucid interval
  • Intracerebral: presents like ischaemic stroke or decreased consciousness; CT shows hyperdense lesion within brain substance

Complications

  • Cerebral oedema
  • Ischaemia
  • Tonsillar or tentorial herniation
  • Infection

Management / Diagnosis

  • GCS assessment: mild (13–15), moderate (9–12), severe (3–8)
  • CT head within 1h: GCS <13, GCS <15 at 2h, suspected open/depressed skull fracture, basal skull fracture signs (haemotympanum, 'panda' eyes, CSF leak, Battle's sign), post-traumatic seizure, focal neurological deficit, >1 episode vomiting
  • CT head within 8h: age ≥65, bleeding/clotting disorder (incl. anticoagulants), dangerous mechanism (pedestrian/cyclist struck by vehicle, fall >1m/5 stairs), >30 min retrograde amnesia
  • Warfarin patients: CT within 8h regardless of other indications
PCSPost-Concussion Syndrome
Persistent symptoms following even minor head trauma

Definition

  • Post-concussion syndrome occurs after even minor head trauma, characterised by a constellation of persistent symptoms.

Aetiology

  • Minor head trauma

Clinical Picture

  • Headache
  • Fatigue
  • Anxiety / depression
  • Dizziness

Complications

  • Persistent symptoms may last weeks to months

Management / Diagnosis

  • Supportive care
  • Reassurance and patient education
  • Graduated return to activity
  • Treat specific symptoms (analgesia for headache, anxiolytics/antidepressants if needed)
LISLocked-in Syndrome
Basilar Artery Occlusion — Conscious but Paralysed

Definition

  • A devastating neurological syndrome caused by basilar artery occlusion resulting in complete paralysis of all voluntary muscles except for eye movements. The patient is fully conscious and aware but unable to speak or move.

Aetiology

  • Basilar artery occlusion
  • Brainstem infarction (ventral pons)

Clinical Picture

  • Complete paralysis of all voluntary muscles
  • Patient is fully conscious and aware
  • Eye movements preserved — patient can communicate via vertical eye movements and blinking
  • Anarthria

Complications

  • High mortality
  • Aspiration pneumonia

Management / Diagnosis

  • Supportive care
  • Communication aids (eye-tracking devices)
  • Treat underlying vascular risk factors
SSSSubclavian Steal Syndrome
Retrograde Vertebral Artery Flow — Arm Ischaemia

Definition

  • Subclavian artery stenosis proximal to the origin of the vertebral artery causes blood to be 'stolen' by retrograde flow down the vertebral artery into the arm, causing brainstem ischaemia after arm use.

Aetiology

  • Subclavian artery stenosis proximal to vertebral artery origin (commonly atherosclerotic)

Clinical Picture

  • Brainstem ischaemia after use of the arm
  • BP differs by >20 mmHg between arms (key diagnostic clue)
  • Vertigo, syncope, or presyncope on arm exertion

Complications

  • Risk of stroke

Management / Diagnosis

  • Suspect if BP in each arm differs by >20 mmHg
  • Doppler ultrasound / angiography to confirm
  • Surgical or endovascular revascularisation (stenting)
TGATransient Global Amnesia
Acute Anterograde Amnesia — Self-limiting within 24h

Definition

  • Characterised by the acute onset of anterograde amnesia (inability to form new memories). Aetiology unknown — thought to be due to transient ischaemia to the thalamus, amygdala, and hippocampus.

Aetiology

  • Unknown aetiology
  • Possible transient ischaemia to the thalamus/hippocampus
  • Often precipitated by physical or emotional stress

Clinical Picture

  • Acute onset of anterograde amnesia — inability to form new memories
  • Patient appears anxious and repeatedly asks the same question
  • Episodes are self-limiting, resolve within 24 hours
  • No loss of personal identity
  • Normal neurological examination

Complications

  • Recurrence is rare

Management / Diagnosis

  • Reassurance — episodes self-limiting
  • Supportive management
  • Exclude other causes (TIA, epilepsy, hypoglycaemia)
  • MRI may show hippocampal diffusion restriction
APHAphasia
Wernicke's, Broca's, Conduction & Global Aphasia

Definition

  • A communication disorder resulting from damage to brain regions responsible for language processing and production. Affects speaking, understanding, reading, and writing.

Aetiology

  • Cerebrovascular accidents (strokes) — most common cause
  • Traumatic brain injury
  • Brain tumours
  • Neurodegenerative diseases

Clinical Picture

  • Wernicke's (Receptive): lesion of superior temporal gyrus (inferior division of left MCA); fluent speech, 'word salad', comprehension impaired
  • Broca's (Expressive): lesion of inferior frontal gyrus (superior division of left MCA); non-fluent, laboured, halting speech; repetition impaired; comprehension normal
  • Conduction: lesion of arcuate fasciculus connecting Wernicke's and Broca's; fluent but repetition poor; patient aware of errors; comprehension normal
  • Global: large lesion affecting all 3 areas; severe expressive and receptive aphasia

Complications

  • Depends on underlying aetiology
  • Significant impact on communication and quality of life

Management / Diagnosis

  • Treat underlying cause (e.g. stroke management)
  • Speech and language therapy
  • MRI to localise lesion

EPGEpilepsy (General)
Chronic neurological disorder of recurrent unprovoked seizures

Definition

  • Chronic neurological disorder defined by recurrent, unprovoked seizures due to abnormal, excessive, synchronous neuronal activity. Diagnosis requires at least two unprovoked seizures separated by >24 hours. Causes include genetic, structural, metabolic, immune, and infectious factors.

Aetiology

  • Genetic
  • Structural (e.g. tumours, cortical dysplasia)
  • Metabolic
  • Immune
  • Infectious (e.g. meningitis, encephalitis)

Clinical Picture

  • Focal seizures: start in one hemisphere; may be focal aware (simple partial), focal impaired awareness (complex partial), or with motor (Jacksonian march) /non-motor (déjà vu) manifestations
  • Generalised seizures: bilateral from onset; consciousness lost immediately; types include tonic-clonic (grand mal), typical absence (petit mal), tonic, clonic, atonic
  • Focal to bilateral (secondary generalised)
  • Temporal lobe: rising epigastric aura, automatisms (lip smacking), déjà vu, olfactory/gustatory hallucinations
  • Frontal lobe: head/leg movements, posturing, post-ictal weakness
  • Parietal lobe: paraesthesia
  • Occipital lobe: floaters/flashes

Complications

  • Injury during seizures
  • SUDEP (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy)
  • Status epilepticus
  • Psychosocial impact (driving restrictions, employment, stigma)

Management / Diagnosis

  • Start AEDs after second seizure; after first if structural abnormality on imaging, EEG epileptic activity, neurological deficit, or unacceptable risk
  • Generalised tonic-clonic: males → sodium valproate; females → lamotrigine or levetiracetam
  • Focal seizures: 1st line lamotrigine or levetiracetam; 2nd line carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, or zonisamide
  • Absence seizures: 1st line ethosuximide; 2nd line sodium valproate (males) /lamotrigine or levetiracetam (females); carbamazepine may exacerbate
  • Myoclonic: males → sodium valproate; females → levetiracetam
  • Tonic/atonic: males → sodium valproate; females → lamotrigine
  • Aim for monotherapy
  • Driving: DVLA must be informed; 12 months seizure-free for licence restoration
EPCEpilepsy in Children
Paediatric epilepsy syndromes

Definition

  • Several specific epilepsy syndromes present in childhood including infantile spasms (West's syndrome), typical absence seizures, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Benign Rolandic epilepsy, and Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (Janz syndrome).

Aetiology

  • Infantile spasms: secondary to serious neurological abnormality (e.g. tuberous sclerosis, encephalitis, birth asphyxia) or idiopathic
  • Lennox-Gastaut: may be extension of infantile spasms
  • Benign Rolandic: childhood-onset, more common in males
  • Janz syndrome: teenage onset, more common in girls

Clinical Picture

  • Infantile spasms (West's): flexion of head/trunk/limbs → extension of arms (salaam attack); 1-2 secs, repeat up to 50 times; progressive mental handicap; EEG: hypsarrhythmia
  • Typical absence (petit mal): onset 4-8 yrs; few-30 secs; no warning, quick recovery; many per day; EEG: 3Hz generalised symmetrical spike-wave
  • Lennox-Gastaut: onset 1-5 yrs; atypical absences, falls, jerks; 90% moderate-severe mental handicap; EEG: slow spike
  • Benign Rolandic: paraesthesia (e.g. unilateral face), usually on waking
  • Juvenile myoclonic (Janz): infrequent generalised seizures (often morning/post sleep deprivation); daytime absences; sudden shock-like myoclonic seizures

Complications

  • Infantile spasms: poor prognosis
  • Lennox-Gastaut: 90% moderate-severe mental handicap

Management / Diagnosis

  • Infantile spasms: vigabatrin and steroids — poor prognosis
  • Typical absence: sodium valproate, ethosuximide — good prognosis (90-95% seizure-free in adolescence)
  • Lennox-Gastaut: ketogenic diet may help
  • Juvenile myoclonic: usually good response to sodium valproate
EPPEpilepsy in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
Management of epilepsy in pregnant and breastfeeding women

Definition

  • Risks of uncontrolled epilepsy during pregnancy generally outweigh risks of medication to the foetus. Congenital defects rise from 1-2% (non-epileptic mothers) to 3-4% with antiepileptic medication.

Aetiology

  • Drug-related teratogenicity is the main concern

Clinical Picture

  • Possible neural tube defects with sodium valproate
  • Cleft palate with phenytoin
  • Neurodevelopmental delay in children following maternal sodium valproate use

Complications

  • Neural tube defects (sodium valproate)
  • Cleft palate (phenytoin)
  • Neurodevelopmental delay (sodium valproate)
  • Congenital malformations (3-4% with any AED)

Management / Diagnosis

  • Advise folic acid 5mg/day well before pregnancy
  • Aim for monotherapy
  • No indication to monitor AED levels routinely
  • Sodium valproate: should not be used during pregnancy unless clearly necessary; avoid in women of childbearing age
  • Carbamazepine: considered least teratogenic of older AEDs
  • Lamotrigine: low malformation rate; dose may need increase in pregnancy
  • Phenytoin: give vitamin K in last month to prevent clotting disorders in newborn
  • Breastfeeding safe except with barbiturates
  • Women of childbearing age should not start valproate without specialist advice
BREBenign Rolandic Epilepsy
Childhood-onset focal epilepsy with excellent prognosis

Definition

  • A form of childhood epilepsy typically occurring between age 4-12 years, more common in males. Seizures characteristically occur at night.

Aetiology

  • Idiopathic; no specific structural cause identified

Clinical Picture

  • Seizures characteristically at night
  • Typically partial: paraesthesia affecting the face
  • Secondary generalisation may occur (parents may only report tonic-clonic movements)
  • Child is otherwise normal
  • EEG: centrotemporal spikes

Complications

  • Generally none — excellent prognosis

Management / Diagnosis

  • Often self-limiting; seizures stop by adolescence

MGMyasthenia gravis
Neuro-muscular

Definition

  • Autoimmune disorder resulting in insufficient functioning acetylcholine receptors. Antibodies to ACh receptors in 85–90%. More common in women (2:1).

Aetiology

  • Autoimmune antibodies to acetylcholine receptors (85–90%)
  • Anti-MuSK antibodies in ~40% of AChR-negative patients
  • Thymoma in 15%; thymic hyperplasia in 50–70%

Clinical Picture

  • Muscle fatigability — progressively weaker during activity, improve after rest
  • Extraocular muscle weakness: diplopia
  • Proximal muscle weakness: face, neck, limb girdle
  • Ptosis
  • Dysphagia
  • Associations: pernicious anaemia, autoimmune thyroid disorders, RA, SLE

Complications

  • Myasthenic crisis (respiratory failure)

Management / Diagnosis

  • Pyridostigmine (long-acting acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) 1st line
  • Immunosuppression: prednisolone initially; azathioprine, cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil
  • Thymectomy
  • Myasthenic crisis: plasmapheresis + IV immunoglobulins
LESLambert-Eaton syndrome
Neuro-muscular

Definition

  • Caused by antibody against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Seen in association with small cell lung cancer and to a lesser extent breast/ovarian cancer. May also occur as autoimmune disorder.

Aetiology

  • Paraneoplastic: small cell lung cancer, breast/ovarian cancer
  • Autoimmune (independent of cancer)

Clinical Picture

  • Repeated muscle contractions increase strength (opposite of MG) — seen in only 50%; after prolonged use strength eventually decreases
  • Limb-girdle weakness (lower limbs first)
  • Hyporeflexia
  • Autonomic symptoms: dry mouth, impotence, difficulty micturating
  • Ophthalmoplegia and ptosis NOT commonly a feature (unlike MG)

Complications

  • Underlying malignancy

Management / Diagnosis

  • Treat underlying cancer
  • Immunosuppression: prednisolone, azathioprine
  • 3,4-diaminopyridine (currently being trialled; blocks K⁺ channel efflux → increased Ca²⁺ channel opening → more ACh release)
  • IVIG and plasma exchange may be beneficial
DYSDystrophinopathies
Muscular Disorders

Definition

  • X-linked recessive disorders due to mutation in dystrophin gene on Xp21. Dystrophin connects muscle membrane to actin. Duchenne = frameshift mutation (severe); Becker = non-frameshift insertion (milder).

Aetiology

  • Duchenne: frameshift mutation → one or both binding sites lost → severe
  • Becker: non-frameshift insertion → both binding sites preserved → milder

Clinical Picture

  • Duchenne: progressive proximal muscle weakness from ~5 years, calf pseudohypertrophy, Gower's sign, intellectual impairment (30%)
  • Becker: develops after age 10, less severe weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy less common, Gower's sign less common, intellectual impairment much less common

Complications

  • Progressive wheelchair dependence (Duchenne)
  • Cardiomyopathy
  • Respiratory failure

Management / Diagnosis

  • Supportive: physiotherapy, orthotics, respiratory support
  • Corticosteroids may slow progression in Duchenne
  • Cardiac monitoring
  • Genetic counselling

Muscular Disorders

Definition

  • Also called dystrophia myotonica (DM). Inherited myopathy affecting skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle. Autosomal dominant trinucleotide repeat disorder. Features develop at ~20–30 years.

Aetiology

  • DM1: CTG repeat at DMPK gene on chromosome 19 (distal weakness more prominent)
  • DM2: repeat expansion of ZNF9 gene on chromosome 3 (proximal weakness more prominent)

Clinical Picture

  • Frontal balding
  • Bilateral ptosis
  • Cataracts
  • Dysarthria
  • Myotonic facies (long, 'haggard' appearance)
  • Myotonia (tonic spasm of muscle)
  • Weakness of arms and legs (distal initially)
  • Cardiac involvement: heart block, cardiomyopathy
  • Mild mental impairment
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Testicular atrophy, dysphagia

Complications

  • Heart block
  • Cardiomyopathy
  • Respiratory failure

Management / Diagnosis

  • Cardiac monitoring (ECG, echocardiogram) — consider pacemaker for heart block
  • Genetic counselling
  • Supportive management

Muscular Disorders

Definition

  • Autosomal dominant form of muscular dystrophy. Affects face, scapula, and upper arms first. Symptoms typically present by age 20.

Aetiology

  • Autosomal dominant

Clinical Picture

  • Facial muscles involved first: difficulty closing eyes, smiling, blowing
  • Weakness of shoulder and upper arm muscles
  • 'Winging' of scapulae
  • Lower limb: hip girdle weakness, foot drop

Complications

  • Progressive muscle weakness

Management / Diagnosis

  • Physiotherapy
  • Supportive management

HSEHerpes Simplex Encephalitis
CNS Infections

Definition

  • Most common cause of sporadic fatal encephalitis, caused by HSV (HSV-1 responsible for 95% of cases in adults). Characteristically affects the temporal lobes and inferior frontal lobes. Presents with acute onset of fever, headache, altered mental status, seizures, and focal neurological deficits. Left untreated mortality approaches 80%.

Aetiology

  • HSV-1 causes 95% of adult cases
  • HSV-2 causes neonatal cases
  • Typically affects temporal and inferior frontal lobes
  • Peripheral cold sores have no relation to HSV encephalitis

Clinical Picture

  • Fever, headache
  • Psychiatric symptoms
  • Seizures, vomiting
  • Focal neurological deficits e.g. aphasia
  • CSF: lymphocytosis, elevated protein
  • PCR for HSV in CSF is diagnostic
  • CT: medial temporal/inferior frontal changes (normal in 1/3); MRI is better
  • EEG: lateralised periodic discharges at 2 Hz

Complications

  • Mortality 80% if untreated; 10-20% if acyclovir started promptly
  • Permanent neurological sequelae common

Management / Diagnosis

  • Intravenous acyclovir — must be started early
ABSBrain Abscess
CNS Infections

Definition

  • A focal intracranial collection of pus that may result from extension of sepsis from middle ear or sinuses, trauma/surgery to the scalp, penetrating head injuries, or embolic events from endocarditis. Has considerable mass effect and raised intracranial pressure is common.

Aetiology

  • Extension of sepsis from middle ear or sinuses
  • Trauma or surgery to the scalp
  • Penetrating head injuries
  • Embolic events from endocarditis

Clinical Picture

  • Headache (often dull, persistent)
  • Fever (may be absent; not swinging pyrexia)
  • Focal neurology e.g. oculomotor/abducens nerve palsy
  • Raised intracranial pressure: nausea, papilloedema, seizures
  • CT scanning for diagnosis

Complications

  • Raised intracranial pressure
  • Seizures
  • Abscess may reform after drainage

Management / Diagnosis

  • IV 3rd-generation cephalosporin + metronidazole
  • Surgical: craniotomy and abscess cavity debridement
  • Intracranial pressure management: e.g. Dexamethasone
ADEMAcute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis
CNS Infections

Definition

  • An autoimmune demyelinating disease of the CNS, also termed post-infectious encephalomyelitis. Can occur following infection with bacterial or viral pathogens including measles, mumps, rubella and varicella. After a lag of days to 2 months, causes acute onset of multifocal neurological symptoms with rapid deterioration.

Aetiology

  • Post-infectious: follows bacterial or viral infection
  • Common preceding infections: measles, mumps, rubella, varicella
  • Also called post-infectious encephalomyelitis

Clinical Picture

  • Lag time of a few days to 2 months post-infection
  • Acute onset of multifocal neurological symptoms with rapid deterioration
  • Headache, fever, nausea, vomiting
  • Motor and sensory deficits
  • Brainstem involvement including oculomotor defects
  • No specific biomarkers; MRI may show supra- and infra-tentorial demyelination

Complications

  • Permanent neurological deficits possible

Management / Diagnosis

  • Intravenous glucocorticoids
  • IVIG where glucocorticoids fail
CJDCreutzfeldt-Jakob Disease
Other Neurological Disorders

Definition

  • A rapidly progressive neurological condition caused by prion proteins that induce amyloid folds forming beta-pleated sheets resistant to proteases. Rapid-onset dementia and myoclonus are characteristic. Sporadic CJD accounts for 85% of cases; 10-15% are familial. Mean age of onset 65 years.

Aetiology

  • Prion proteins inducing amyloid folds resistant to proteases
  • Sporadic CJD: 85% of cases, mean onset 65 years
  • Familial CJD: 10-15% of cases
  • New variant CJD: average onset 25 years; linked to BSE
  • Methionine homozygosity at codon 129 of prion protein is a risk factor

Clinical Picture

  • Rapid-onset dementia
  • Myoclonus
  • New variant: anxiety, withdrawal, dysphonia most common
  • CSF: usually normal
  • EEG: biphasic, high amplitude sharp waves (sporadic CJD only)
  • MRI: hyperintense signals in basal ganglia and thalamus

Complications

  • Median survival 13 months (new variant)
  • Fatality is universal

Management / Diagnosis

  • No curative treatment available
  • Supportive care only

Definition

  • Features develop after 35 years

  • Dystonia
  • Personality changes (irritability, apathy, depression)

Complications

  • Death ~20 years after symptom onset

Management / Diagnosis

  • No cure; supportive management
